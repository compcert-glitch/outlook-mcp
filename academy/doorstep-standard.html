01

Behaviour is a technical competence, not a soft one

In energy efficiency, retrofit and building pathology, we are almost never the bearer of good news. A correct CR3 on a damp wall is worth nothing if the occupant stops listening at the word “mould”, the landlord’s agent gets defensive, and the report is filed unread.

Our surveyors work alone, in other people’s homes, delivering findings that cost people money, delay their sale, or confirm that the place they live in is making them unwell. That is emotional labour performed four or five times a day, in a car, without a colleague to decompress with. The skill of doing it well is learnable and teachable — and it is currently learned by accident, by watching whoever a new surveyor happened to shadow.

The gap this closes

Nearly every complaint we would recognise turns on how a finding landed, not whether it was right. Tone, pace, and who felt talked down to.

The risk it manages

A surveyor who softens a finding to keep the peace on a doorstep has created a professional indemnity exposure. Candour is the risk control.

The people it keeps

Field surveyors leave over accumulated emotional load far more often than over pay. Debriefs and peer support are a retention mechanism.

Where it connects to our professional obligations

This is not a wellbeing bolt-on. It sits directly under the RICS Rules of Conduct: honesty and integrity (Rule 1), good-quality and diligent service (Rule 3), treating others with respect and encouraging diversity and inclusion (Rule 4), and acting in the public interest and preventing harm (Rule 5). It maps to the mandatory APC competencies in Client care, Communication and negotiation, Conflict avoidance and dispute resolution, and Diversity, inclusion and teamworking — so APC candidates can evidence it directly.

Design principle running through the whole programme: we never train people to be nicer about a finding. We train them to be clearer about it, sooner, with the evidence in front of both parties. Warmth is the delivery mechanism; the finding itself never moves.

02

The six behaviours

One framework, used everywhere: recruitment scoring, probation reviews, ride-along feedback, the annual conversation, and promotion to Senior. Three levels, because a Foundation surveyor and a Team Lead are not doing the same thing with the same skill.

Behaviour Foundation — assistant, trainee, APC candidate Practising — chartered surveyor Leading — senior, team lead, mentor
Steady under pressureSelf-management Recognises own stress signals and uses a reset before speaking. Does not mirror an angry occupant’s volume. Stays regulated through a full confrontation and still closes the visit properly. Reschedules rather than pushing through when unfit to inspect. Is the calmest voice on the call. Absorbs escalation from a client without passing the pressure down to the surveyor who is already shaken.
Read the roomSocial awareness Notices who is actually in the property and what state they are in before starting the inspection. Adjusts language, pace and route through the property to the occupant — vulnerability, language, faith, disability, children present. Spots the dynamic a surveyor has missed (who really holds the decision, who is being spoken over) and names it in the debrief.
Straight talking, kindly doneCandour with care States the finding in plain words without jargon or hedging. Does not promise outcomes that are not ours to give. Delivers an unwelcome, expensive or health-related finding using LEVEL, in person, before it appears in writing. Holds the line on a finding under commercial pressure from a repeat client, and is seen to do it.
Curious before certainProfessional scepticism Asks the occupant about the history of the defect before forming a view. Records what was said. Actively tests the competing hypothesis — condensation vs. penetrating vs. rising, ventilation vs. fabric — and notices when fatigue is doing the diagnosing. Builds the habit into the team: asks “what would have to be true for this to be something else?” in case clinics.
Own itAccountability Raises an access failure, a missed area or an error the same day rather than hoping it holds. Corrects own report promptly and without defensiveness. Says “I got that wrong” to a client without collapsing. Makes it survivable to admit a mistake. Responds to a reported error with the fix first and the cause second.
Lift othersTeam & dispersed working Uses the peer line after a hard visit instead of sitting on it. Answers a colleague’s call. Debriefs juniors after ride-alongs with specifics, not reassurance. Shares the visits that went badly, not only the clean ones. Runs the regional case clinic. Knows which of their surveyors is carrying the heaviest emotional load this month.

Note on “Curious before certain”: this is the behaviour that earns the programme its credibility with a technical audience. Diagnostic anchoring — deciding it is rising damp because three of today’s four were — is an emotional failure with a technical consequence. Self-awareness and building pathology are the same discipline here.

03

Emotional condition ratings

Surveyors already triage buildings on a three-point scale. The programme borrows the same grammar for the human side of the visit, so it needs no new vocabulary — a surveyor can flag an ECR3 visit to a coordinator in one character.

ECR 1

Routine

Cooperative occupant, expectations broadly accurate, findings unsurprising. The emotional work is maintaining quality on the fourth visit of the day.

What we trainConsistency. The doorstep script does not degrade at 4pm.

ECR 2

Managed

A gap to close: expectations don’t match reality, money or blame is in play, or a third party is pushing. Manageable in the moment with structure.

What we trainLEVEL, expectation resets, and holding a finding under challenge.

ECR 3

Serious — flag it

Health, safeguarding, hostility, or a finding that changes someone’s living situation. Beyond one surveyor’s judgement alone.

What we trainStabilise, record factually, escalate the same day, and debrief before the next appointment.

An ECR3 is not a mark against a surveyor — it is a request for support and the trigger for a same-day check-in from the team lead. Getting that culturally right is the single most important thing to land in the first cohort. If ECR3s stay under-reported, the rest of the programme is decoration.

04

The six modules

One half-day a month, regionally delivered, cohorts of ten to twelve so everybody speaks. Each module follows the same shape: a real anonymised case from our own files, the tool, live practice in threes with a genuinely difficult brief, then one commitment each to test in the field before the next session.

01Month 1

The load you are carrying

Self-awareness — what the job does to you, and what that does to your surveying

  • The emotional profile of a survey day: lone working, back-to-back appointments, the report backlog, the phone call you are dreading.
  • Your own tells — what you do at the point of stress. Clipped sentences, over-explaining, rushing the loft, agreeing with the client to get out of the house.
  • The direct line from depleted to defensive to a weaker report.
  • Tool taught: Notice / Name / Next sentence.
3h formal CPDRules of Conduct 2, 3Field task: log your ECR ratings for a fortnight
02Month 2

Reading the home, reading the person

Social awareness — the doorstep, vulnerability and inclusive practice

  • The first ninety seconds: how the visit’s emotional weather is set before you have taken a single reading.
  • Consent and control in someone’s home — asking for the route, not just the entry; what it feels like to have a stranger photograph your bedroom.
  • Recognising vulnerability without diagnosing it: fuel poverty and a cold home in February, hoarding, cognitive impairment, fear of the landlord, children in the property.
  • Our safeguarding route: observe, record factually, refer to the named lead the same day, never promise an outcome.
  • Inclusive practice as a Rule 4 obligation, not an optional courtesy — interpreters, faith and household norms, disability, who you address in a room of three people.
3h formal CPDRule 4 · Rule 5APC: Client care, Inclusive environments
03Month 3

Telling people what they don’t want to hear

The core module — unwelcome findings, cost shocks and health risk

  • Why hedging is the most expensive habit in the practice: the occupant hears reassurance, the report says otherwise, and the gap becomes the complaint.
  • Damp and mould in an occupied home — delivering a health-relevant finding to the person living with it, with a child in the doorway. Where social housing is involved, the finding starts a statutory clock, so precision and same-day reporting matter more than comfort.
  • The retrofit cost conversation: when the measure the customer has set their heart on is the wrong measure for the fabric.
  • Grant-funded work and the expectation gap: the occupant who believes the assessment is the installation.
  • Tool taught: LEVEL.
3h formal CPDRule 1 · Rule 3APC: Communication & negotiation
04Month 4

Under fire

Challenge, conflict and complaint — holding a finding without hardening

  • Who challenges us and why: the vendor losing a sale, the agent losing a fee, the contractor whose work we have just failed, the landlord facing a bill, the homeowner who has already spent the money.
  • Separating the finding (not negotiable) from the route to fixing it (very negotiable).
  • De-escalation on a doorstep, and the point at which you leave. Lone-working safety overrides completing the survey, every time.
  • The hostile email six weeks later. First response drafting: acknowledge, state what we did, set the review route, never argue by email.
  • Tool taught: Hold the finding, move the person.

05Month 5

Curious before certain

Where emotion distorts technical judgement

  • Anchoring, pattern-matching and fatigue in defect diagnosis — the fourth damp inspection of the day is the dangerous one.
  • Deference and its opposite: softening a rating because the client is a major account; hardening one because you were doubted last week.
  • Interviewing the occupant as evidence-gathering. The history of the defect is data, and most surveyors collect it badly.
  • Practice: two surveyors, same photographs and readings, different conclusions — work out what drove the difference.

Lifting the team

Peer support and mentoring across a dispersed field force

  • The two-minute debrief, and why it happens in the car before the next appointment, not at the end of the month.
  • Coaching a shaken colleague without either minimising it or catastrophising it.
  • Mentoring APC candidates on behaviour, not just technical competence — what to feed back after a ride-along.

05

Field tools

Four tools, printed on one laminated card for the van. Everything else in the programme is practice for these.

The first ninety seconds

Arrival. The single highest-leverage moment of the visit, and the one we currently leave to personality.

  1. 1
    Knock, then step backPut a pace between you and the door before it opens. A stranger filling a doorway reads as threat; a stranger standing back reads as expected.
  2. 2
    Name, firm, purpose, duration, ID — in that orderSay how long you will be before they have to ask. Uncertainty about duration is the most common source of doorstep tension.“Morning — James Whitfield, Crawford and CO, here for the retrofit assessment. I’ll be about ninety minutes. Here’s my ID.”
  3. 3
    Ask for the route, not just the entryTell them where you need to go and in what order, and ask if that works. It returns control of the home to the person who lives in it.
  4. 4
    Notice one thing about the person, not the buildingYou are about to spend ninety minutes assessing their home. Thirty seconds acknowledging them first pays for itself at the findings conversation.“Looks like I’ve caught you mid school run — I can start outside and come in at half past, if that’s easier.”

LEVEL — delivering an unwelcome finding

Use in person, before it reaches them in writing. Roughly three minutes. Never deliver a serious finding in a doorway on the way out.

  1. L
    Lead with the headlineOne sentence, plain words, no preamble. Preamble is heard as bad news being smuggled in, and it costs you their trust before you have started.“There’s active penetrating damp in the rear bedroom wall, and the mould is a health issue for whoever sleeps in there.”
  2. E
    Evidence, alongside themShow, don’t assert. Stand next to them looking at the wall, the reading, the photo — not opposite them. The evidence should sit between the two of you and the problem, not between you and them.
  3. V
    Validate the impact before they argue for itName what this actually means for them. If you say it first, they don’t have to fight you to be heard.“This is expensive and it’s landing at a terrible time. I’d be sick about it too.”
  4. E
    Explain the options — at least twoIncluding, honestly, the consequence of doing nothing. One option is a verdict; two options is advice. Give ranges, not invented precision, and be clear about what sits outside our scope.
  5. L
    Leave a next stepWho does what, by when — and one of those things is yours. Nobody should be left holding an unwelcome finding with nothing to do about it.“My report is with you Thursday. The section on ventilation is the bit to show your builder. Ring me when you’ve read it — I’d rather talk it through than have you guess.”

Hold the finding, move the person

When a contractor, agent, landlord or client pushes back on a rating. The aim is not to win; it is to end with the finding intact and the relationship workable.

  1. 1
    Say their case back better than they made itUntil they believe you have understood the objection, nothing else you say is being processed.
  2. 2
    Split the two argumentsThe finding is not negotiable. The route to fixing it, the sequencing, the cost, the phasing — genuinely negotiable, and that is where you spend the goodwill.
  3. 3
    Put the evidence on the table, not between youLiterally move to the same side. Readings, photographs, the standard you are applying, in front of both of you.
  4. 4
    Offer the route, not the retreatA second opinion, a re-inspection, our formal review process. All legitimate. Changing a rating to end an argument is not.“If your builder disagrees, have him put it in writing and I’ll come back out and look at it with him. What I can’t do is take it out of the report because it’s inconvenient.”
  5. 5
    Record it the same dayWhat was challenged, by whom, what you said. If it is going to become a complaint, your note is written now, not reconstructed in four months.

The two-minute debrief

After any ECR3 visit, in the car, before the engine goes back on. To a peer pair or the team lead — voice note is fine.

  1. 1
    What actually happened?Facts only. Sixty seconds. Saying it out loud is most of the work.
  2. 2
    What am I carrying out of it?Anger, guilt, worry about the child in that bedroom, doubt about a reading. Name it so it does not travel into the next appointment.
  3. 3
    What needs doing today, by whom?Safeguarding referral, a call to the coordinator, a note on file, or simply nothing — and the peer confirming that nothing is genuinely the right answer.
06

Scenario library

The teaching material. Each is run in threes — surveyor, other party, observer with the behaviours card — then rotated so everyone plays the difficult party. Facilitators should replace these with anonymised real cases from our own files as the library matures; nothing teaches like a case the room recognises.

ECR 3

The third report of the same mould

SituationBlack mould to the external wall of a child’s bedroom in a social tenancy. The tenant tells you she has reported it three times. The managing agent has come to the visit and is standing behind you.
What is really going onThe tenant expects to be disbelieved again and is testing whether you are on the agent’s side. The agent is managing exposure. You are the first person in the room whose job is to say what is true.
What good looks likeAddress the tenant, not the agent, and say the finding out loud in front of both. Name the health relevance plainly. Record the reporting history as evidence, not as complaint. Same-day write-up, because the clock on this is not ours to reset.
The trapTalking to the agent because the agent is the client. The tenant reads it instantly and stops giving you information you need.
ECR 3

Eighteen thousand pounds, already spent

SituationExternal wall insulation to a 1930s solid-wall semi, installed two years ago by another firm. Detailing has failed, moisture is trapped, and the interstitial risk is now worse than before the work. The owner is proud of it.
What is really going onYou are not delivering a technical finding, you are telling someone they were taken advantage of. Shame arrives before anger, and shame is what makes people argue with evidence.
What good looks likeLEVEL, slowly. Separate the decision from the person — the decision was reasonable on the information they had. Be specific about what can be recovered and what cannot. Point to the routes available to them without becoming their advocate or their solicitor.
The trapTechnical detail as an anaesthetic. Twenty minutes on vapour permeability so you don’t have to say the sentence.
ECR 3

No heating on in February

SituationAn elderly owner, house at roughly nine degrees, one room in use, an unopened pile of post. She is apologetic and changes the subject whenever you ask about the boiler.
What is really going onAlmost certainly fuel poverty, possibly with the boiler failed and unreported. The subject-changing is dignity, not evasion.
What good looks likeAsk about warmth, not money. Keep the observation factual on file — internal temperature, heating not in use, occupant declined to discuss. Refer to our safeguarding lead the same day. Mention available support without turning yourself into the caseworker or promising anything we cannot deliver.
The trapEither sailing past it because it is not in scope, or over-promising a grant you cannot guarantee. Both do harm.
ECR 2

“So when do the lads start?”

SituationGrant-funded retrofit assessment. Halfway round, it becomes clear the occupant believes today is the installation, that everything is free, and that it finishes this week.
What is really going onAn expectation gap created upstream of you, which you have inherited and will be blamed for. Every minute you let it run makes the correction more painful.
What good looks likeStop and reset immediately, before the loft. Explain what today is, what comes next, what is funded and what may not be, and who decides. Write down the sequence and leave it with them. Flag the mis-set expectation back upstream so the next one is better.
The trapFinishing the survey and letting the report break the news. That is the complaint, written in advance.
ECR 2

“My builder says it’s fine”

SituationClient disputes your CR3 on the roof covering because a builder they trust has told them it has years left. The sale depends on your rating.
What is really going onYou are the obstacle between them and a completed transaction. The builder is being used as a lever, not quoted as an expert.
What good looks likeHold the finding, move the person. Restate their case, split the finding from the route, show the evidence side by side, offer re-inspection with the builder present. Record the challenge on file the same day.
The trapSmall softening of the wording “to be reasonable”. That is the sentence that gets read out later.
ECR 2

The flank wall you cannot reach

SituationA heavily hoarded property. Two rooms are impassable, the flank wall is inaccessible, and the occupant is watching your face very closely as you come down the stairs.
What is really going onThey are braced for disgust. Whatever you do with your face in the next three seconds sets the rest of the visit and determines whether you get a second appointment.
What good looks likeNeutral face, ordinary voice, task language only. State the access limitation as a fact about the survey, not a judgement about them. Record limitations precisely. Consider whether a safeguarding referral is warranted, on the same factual basis as any other observation.
The trapOvercompensating with jokes or excessive reassurance. It reads as pity and confirms exactly what they were afraid of.
ECR 2

The agent who wants to walk you round

SituationThe vendor’s estate agent arrives “to help”, keeps up a running commentary, steers you away from the extension, and mentions twice how much work they put our way.
What is really going onSoft interference with an independent inspection, delivered pleasantly enough that objecting feels rude. That is what makes it effective.
What good looks likeFriendly, early, and unmistakable: you inspect alone, you will find them when you are done, and you will look at everything. Said in the first two minutes it costs nothing; said in the twentieth it is a confrontation.
The trapPoliteness as compliance. Rule 1 does not bend for a referral source.
ECR 2

The email six weeks later

SituationA purchase has fallen through. The email is long, hostile, copied to two other people, and questions your competence directly.
What is really going onReal loss looking for somewhere to land. Roughly ten per cent of it is a substantive point that deserves a proper answer.
What good looks likeDo not reply the same hour. Acknowledge within our stated timescale, set out what was inspected and on what basis, name the formal review route, and offer to talk. Find the ten per cent and answer it honestly — including if it is a fair hit.
The trapThe point-by-point rebuttal written at half past ten at night. It never helps and it is always disclosable.
ECR 2

The call from the driveway

SituationA trainee rings you from outside a property. They have been shouted at and asked to leave, and they are trying very hard to sound fine about it.
What is really going onThey are asking whether they did something wrong and whether you are about to be disappointed in them. The technical question is a cover.
What good looks likeSafety first and explicitly — are you out, are you in the car. Then the debrief, in order: what happened, what are you carrying, what needs doing today. Reassign the visit yourself. Feedback, if any, comes tomorrow.
The trapGoing straight to “what did you say to them?”. That is the question that teaches them not to ring next time.
ECR 1

The fourth damp inspection of the day

SituationNobody is upset. Nothing is contentious. It is ten past four, the readings look familiar, and you have written this paragraph three times already today.
What is really going onThe lowest-emotion visit on the list carries the highest diagnostic risk. Fatigue plus pattern-matching is how the wrong cause gets confidently recorded.
What good looks likeName the fatigue rather than pushing through it. Run the competing hypothesis deliberately — what would have to be true for this to be condensation? Take the extra reading you would have taken at nine in the morning.
The trapAssuming emotional intelligence is only for difficult visits. It is doing its most valuable work here.
07

Embedding it

Six half-days on their own will change nothing. Four routines carry it into the field, and they matter more than the training days themselves.

Monthly — 60 minutes, regional, online

The case clinic

One real case, brought by a surveyor, run against the behaviours. Team leads facilitate. The rule that makes it work: the person bringing the case chooses one they handled badly, and leads open by going first for the first three months.

Twice yearly — per surveyor

Behaviour ride-along

A senior observes a live visit against the observation card and feeds back within 24 hours. Explicitly separate from technical audit, and never scored in the same conversation — mixing them turns both into a performance review.

Standing — all field staff

Peer pairs

Everyone leaves Module 6 paired for twelve months, cross-region, with an agreed expectation that either can ring the other after a hard visit. Re-paired annually so the network widens.

Every 1:1 — team leads

Three standing questions

Which visit stayed with you this month? Where did you hold a finding under pressure? Who have you rung, and who has rung you? Three minutes, every time, regardless of workload.

09

Rollout

Nine months from decision to full field coverage, running two cohorts in parallel from month four. Seventy-five surveyors at twelve per cohort is roughly seven cohorts.

Weeks 1–4

Baseline and build

Pull the nine measures. Add the two client-survey questions. Anonymise six real cases from the complaints log and the file room to replace the starter scenarios. Name the safeguarding lead in writing and confirm the referral route actually works end to end.

Weeks 5–8

Train the team leads first

Leads run the full six modules compressed into two days, then are signed off to facilitate. This is the load-bearing decision: if leads treat the framework as HR paperwork, the field will too. Their own 1:1s start using the three standing questions immediately.

Months 3–8

Cohort 1 — deliberately mixed

Twelve people spanning sceptics, high performers and two APC candidates. Not the willing volunteers. The sceptics’ verdict at month six is what determines uptake across the other six cohorts, so recruit for it openly.

Month 6

Review before scaling

Cohort 1 rewrites the scenario library from what they actually met in the field. Cut anything that did not survive contact. Two cohorts then run in parallel.

Months 4–12

Cohorts 2–7 and induction

The framework enters recruitment scoring, induction and probation, so new surveyors meet it in week one rather than year two. Ride-alongs and case clinics run continuously from this point.

Month 12

Measure against baseline

Full read of the nine measures against the pattern described above, plus an honest question to the field: which of the four tools do you actually use? Anything nobody uses gets cut rather than re-taught.

What it needs from us

  • Time, mostly. Eighteen hours per surveyor of chargeable time, plus facilitation and two ride-alongs a year per person. That is the real cost and it should be stated plainly to the board rather than buried.
  • An MD who goes first. The behaviour that gets copied is the one leadership demonstrates under pressure — particularly “Own it”. A visible example from the top in Cohort 1 is worth more than the whole of Module 1.
  • A credible facilitator. Someone who has personally been shouted at on a doorstep. A trainer who has never carried a damp meter will not survive the first session with this audience.
  • No link to pay in year one. The moment the behaviours framework feeds a bonus, the observation card becomes a negotiation and the honest self-assessment stops.
